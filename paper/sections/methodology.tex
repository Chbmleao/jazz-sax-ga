\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

Like AMUSE \cite{ozcan2008}, our system uses a genetic algorithm (GA) to generate an improvised melody over a given harmony. Here, the melody is a jazz tenor saxophone line over a fixed ii-V-I progression in C major, Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 | Cmaj7, played in a loop. Since the chord of each bar is known, every rule can be checked bar by bar. We chose the tenor saxophone because the Weimar Jazz Database has mostly tenor solos (158 tenor against 80 alto), so the statistics we borrow from it fit this instrument \cite{pfleiderer2017}.

\subsection{Representation}

With the problem scoped, we need a representation of a melody that the GA can manipulate. A melody is a genome, an array of integers with one gene per eighth note, so its length depends on the number of bars, which is a parameter. Each integer is either a MIDI pitch from 44 to 76 (the tenor saxophone range in concert pitch), a rest (43), or a hold (77) that sustains the previous note, the same rest and hold idea used in \cite{ozcan2008, matic2010}. The eighth note is the shortest duration and longer notes are built from holds, so there are no triplets or sixteenth notes.

Unlike AMUSE, whose genes are scale degrees, our genes are MIDI pitches, so the GA can produce notes outside the scale, such as the chromatic approach notes that are important in jazz improvisation. When a genome is decoded, consecutive equal pitches are separate notes, since only a hold extends a note. Notes are also split at bar lines, so each note belongs to a single chord, and a hold at the very start of the melody is treated as a rest.

\subsection{Fitness Function}

The fitness of a melody combines five rules. Each rule $i$ gives a score $s_i$ in $[0, 1]$, where 1 is best, and has a weight $w_i$ that sets how much the rule counts in the final fitness. The fitness $F$ is the weighted mean of the scores (Equation~\ref{eq:fitness}). The weights are a parameter of the GA, and in this paper all of them are equal to 1, chosen by hand, which is a limitation of this work.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fitness}
  F = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{5} w_i\, s_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{5} w_i}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Breathing.} A saxophone player has to stop to breathe, so phrases cannot be too long. We measure the longest run of eighths played without a rest. The score is 1 if this run is at most 16 eighths (two bars), and it decreases linearly above that. The limit of 16 eighths is our own choice.
  \item \emph{Step size.} Jazz solos move mostly by small intervals. Ignoring rests and repeated pitches, the score is the mean of two parts: how close the mean interval is to 2.7 semitones, $\max(0, 1 - |\bar{d} - 2.7| / 2.7)$, and how close the share of leaps larger than 7 semitones is to 2\%, $1 - \max(0, p - 0.02) / 0.98$. Both reference values come from the Weimar Jazz Database \cite{pfleiderer2017}.
  \item \emph{Chord tones.} Improvisers usually play chord tones on the beat, as in AMUSE \cite{ozcan2008}. The score is the fraction of beats (even eighths) whose sounding note belongs to the chord of that bar, and beats with a rest are not counted.
  \item \emph{Chromatic approach.} A note outside the C major scale is correct only if it starts off the beat and the next note is one half step away, as in a bebop chromatic approach. The score is the fraction of outside notes that are correct, and 1 if there are none.
  \item \emph{Guide tones.} At each chord change, the first note of the new bar should be the 3rd or the 7th of the new chord, which gives a smooth voice leading between chords \cite{smither2019}. A rest at a chord change counts as a miss.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Genetic Operators}

The GA runs the steps below, in this order, with the default values in Table~\ref{tab:params}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Initialization.} Each melody of the first population is a random walk. It starts at a random pitch, and each eighth is a rest with probability 0.2, a hold with probability 0.1, or else the pitch moves by at most 3 semitones. We first tried a uniform random initialization, but it gave a mean interval of about 9 semitones, and the step score got stuck at about 0.29, because a mutation of $\pm 2$ semitones cannot fix leaps that large.
  \item \emph{Elitism.} The 2 best individuals are copied to the next generation unchanged, so the best fitness never decreases.
  \item \emph{Selection.} Parents are chosen by roulette wheel, with a probability proportional to their fitness.
  \item \emph{Crossover.} One-point crossover at a random bar line, which gives two children. Cutting at a bar line keeps each bar intact, so its notes stay aligned with its chord and guide tones.
  \item \emph{Mutation.} Each eighth of each child mutates with probability 0.01. A mutated eighth becomes a rest (probability 0.2), a hold (0.1), or else its pitch moves by up to $\pm 2$ semitones. A rest or hold that mutates into a pitch gets a random pitch instead.
  \item \emph{Stopping criterion.} The GA stops after a fixed number of generations (1250).
\end{itemize}

The best melody is rendered as MIDI, with the tenor saxophone over a piano that plays one voicing per bar, at 170 BPM, the median tempo in the Weimar Jazz Database \cite{pfleiderer2017}, with a swing ratio of 1.3 \cite{corcoran2021}.
