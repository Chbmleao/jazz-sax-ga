\section{Discussion and Future Work}\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that a low mutation rate works best for this problem. Since the mutation is applied to each eighth, a rate of 0.2 changes about one eighth in five in every child, so good bars are destroyed faster than selection can keep them. This also explains the low mean fitness of the population at 0.2. The chromatic approach rule suffers the most, because a correct approach note depends on two notes in specific positions, which a high mutation rate easily breaks.

A fitness close to 1.0, however, does not mean that the melody is good music. The final melodies with mutation rate 0.01 score 1.0 on the chromatic approach rule, but some of their notes still sounded out of place. Each rule checks the notes locally, so a melody can follow all of them and still sound wrong as a whole. This agrees with Wiggins et al. \cite{wiggins1998}, who note that their systems have neither a clear plan nor intention, and that large-scale structure cannot be expected from a GA with little domain knowledge.

This work also has limitations. The progression and the key are fixed (a ii-V-I in C major), and the melodies only use eighth notes, with no triplets or sixteenths. The rule weights are all equal and were chosen by hand. Every saxophone note is also played with the same MIDI velocity, so the melodies sound robotic, while in real jazz the intensity of each note varies a lot, and this is one of the ways the soloist expresses feelings through the music. Finally, the listening test was informal, with a single listener who was not blind to the generation of each melody.

As future work, the first step is a blind listening test: play the best melody of the first and of the last generation to several listeners, in random order and without saying which is which, and ask which one they prefer. This follows the Turing test of AMUSE \cite{ozcan2008}, where listeners had to tell the solos of the GA apart from the solos of a human. Other extensions are to vary the velocity of each note, to support other progressions, keys and rhythms, and to tune the rule weights instead of choosing them by hand.
